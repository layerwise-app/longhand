Every dry, sandy, disappointing chocolate chip cookie in this country was perfect at some point, and then somebody gave it two more minutes to be safe.

I've watched it happen at Halfmoon with people who get paid to know better. A new hire opens the oven at ten minutes, sees a pale, puffy center, gets nervous, and shuts the door again. That door just cost us the tray. She can't be blamed for it, because at that moment the cookie looks raw. A hot cookie always looks raw. There are two reasons, and once you know them you can stop being afraid.

The first is sugar. Inside a hot cookie, a lot of the sugar is dissolved into a syrup, and hot syrup is runny. The butter is liquid too, since it melts in the mid-90s. As the cookie cools, the syrup stiffens and the butter firms up, and the thing that flopped off your spatula is holding its shape twenty minutes later. Anyone who has made caramel knows this already. What pours like honey in the pan will crack a tooth by dinner. Softness tells you almost nothing about a cookie that is still hot, and softness is the only thing you were checking.

The second is carryover. You know this from a roast chicken, which you pull from the oven and which keeps climbing five or ten degrees on the board. A cookie is too thin to store much heat in its own body, but it's sitting on a sheet of metal that just came out of a 350-degree oven, and the outside of the cookie is hotter than the middle. All of that keeps pouring inward. One slow morning I stuck a thin probe into a cookie pulled at ten minutes. The center read 176 on the way out of the oven and 186 about ninety seconds later, on the pan, on my counter, with the oven nowhere near it. The bottom kept browning. It went right on baking without anybody's permission, and that is your two minutes.

Before anyone writes to me about eggs: 176 is well past the 160 you've been told to reach. That center only looks raw.

## What you're actually saving

Water. I ran this properly because I am the kind of person who owns two scales. Same dough, same pan, 55-gram scoops. The ones I pulled at ten minutes weighed 51 grams once cool, and the ones I pulled at twelve weighed 49.5.

A gram and a half sounds like nothing. But that scoop of dough starts with only about eight grams of water in it. The ten-minute cookie keeps around four. The twelve-minute cookie keeps two and a half, so those two minutes took more than a third of what was left, and that was the third doing the work. Water and dissolved sugar are what make a cookie bend. Take them out and you have made a very buttery cracker.

I left both batches in a tin for three days and gave one of each to Ruben, who delivers our flour and has no patience for me. The ten-minute cookie he ate in silence while reading an invoice. The twelve-minute cookie he snapped in half, looked at, and asked if I was all right.

## The method

1. Portion by weight, or at least by scoop. If your cookies are different sizes, no single timer can be right for all of them.
2. Use a plain, light-colored aluminum half-sheet with parchment. A dark pan browns the bottoms faster, and then you're pulling early for a different reason.
3. Take the low end of your recipe's baking time, subtract two minutes, and set the timer for that.
4. Open the door and look at the edges, not the middle. You want edges that are set and barely golden, and a center that is puffed, pale, and a little glossy. If this feels like a mistake, you're doing it correctly.
5. Leave the cookies on the hot pan for ten full minutes. Do not poke them. They are busy.
6. Move them to a rack only when they lift off in one piece.
7. Let the pan cool completely before the next round. Dough on a warm sheet starts melting before it sees the oven, and you get lace.

A few honest limits. This is for cookies you want chewy. Shortbread, gingersnaps, and biscotti want to be dry, so leave them be. A giant bakery-size cookie needs its own test, because the middle has further to go. And if you've never checked your oven with an eight-dollar thermometer, do that first, since most home ovens I've met are lying by fifteen degrees or more.

Also use better butter. It has nothing to do with this argument. I just say it whenever I have the floor.

My mother has baked her cookies for fourteen minutes since 1981, and I have shown her the numbers. Last Christmas I stood in her kitchen with the same recipe card she's always used, and at ten minutes she went off to look for her glasses, and I opened the oven. She ate one of those cookies three days later, standing at the counter, and chewed for a while, and said this is what happens when you finally spring for the good butter.

She had bought the same butter as always. I told her she was probably right.
